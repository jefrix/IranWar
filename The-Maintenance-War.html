The Maintenance
War

Brent above $100 for the third time this year. Why the Iran escalation is a maintenance cycle rather than an event, and what four channels of chokepoint load look like from the inside.

Published September 9, 2026
Reading time ~22 minutes
Filed under Geopolitics / Energy / Systems
Part of Volume Three

On the morning of September ninth, Brent crude traded at one hundred two dollars and five cents a barrel — up two dollars and twenty cents from the previous morning, and roughly thirty-five dollars above the same date last year. The trigger was not a blockade, not a minefield, not a declaration. It was five tankers. Iranian crude carriers, destroyed by American aircraft in the Gulf of Oman, in retaliation for ballistic missiles the Revolutionary Guard had fired at a U.S. Navy warship. Central Command released footage of one of them, the M/T Riesco, going down.

Iran answered by striking a U.S. base in Jordan, which reported intercepting eighteen of twenty incoming missiles. The Houthis claimed strikes on the four-hundred-thousand-barrel-a-day Jazan refinery in Saudi Arabia, with more than seventy wounded. By midday roughly seven million barrels a day were still moving through the Strait of Hormuz, against the twenty million that passed before the war began.

This is the third time the price has crossed a hundred this year — March ninth, July twenty-third, and now. Each crossing has been reported as an event: a spike, a shock, a headline that resolves by Friday. It is not an event. It is a maintenance cycle, and the difference is the whole argument.

Chapter I

The Doctrine of Resumption, Running Again

Volume Two closed on the observation that this war had been declared over twice and resumed twice — that an innovation used once is an expedient and an innovation used twice is a doctrine. Dispatch №20 named the pattern. №21 watched it harden into a reliability standard. №22 watched the underwriter state absorb the cost. None of those dispatches predicted a third resumption. They described a grammar, and grammars do not need predicting. They need only be read.

The June memorandum of understanding was the second termination. Brent fell to sixty-nine dollars on July second. The calm did not survive the month: renewed attacks on tankers transiting Hormuz pushed the spot price to one hundred five by July twenty-third, then back to the mid-eighties through August. What changed in the first week of September was the target set. The earlier strikes hit military assets. These hit commercial hulls — the shadow-fleet carriers Central Command identifies as the funding network behind the Guard and its regional allies.

That widening is the doctrine's signature. Each resumption enlarges the category of legitimate target, and each enlargement is narrated afterward as a response to the other side.

Except this time it was narrated in advance. Admiral Brad Cooper, the CENTCOM commander, gave the exchange rate publicly: shoot at two of ours and we will impose a higher economic cost by destroying three of yours. A published ratio is not a response to escalation. It is a schedule for it. Every subsequent widening stops being an improvised reaction and becomes the execution of an announced policy. The grammar has stopped hiding.

The market's response is the second tell. Daan Struyven, co-head of global commodities research at Goldman Sachs, told Bloomberg Television on the seventh that Brent could reach one hundred twenty in a scenario where shipping attacks broaden and intensify — and could fall toward eighty if regional exports normalize. A forty-dollar spread is a bank declining to forecast. It is also a bank saying, in the only language it has, that the price of oil is now a function of whether a grammar keeps running rather than of anything on a balance sheet.

Chapter II

What a Chokepoint Actually Does

The Late Bronze Age collapse did not arrive as a catastrophe. It arrived as a sequence of narrow failures in systems that had forgotten they were narrow. Ugarit's last tablets are not dramatic; they are administrative. A king writing to another king that his ships are away, his troops are in Hatti, his coast is unguarded. An unfired tablet promising grain that never shipped. The fire that destroyed the palace baked the clay hard enough to preserve the paperwork of the final weeks. Every request for help was answered with another request for help. That is what institutional failure looks like from the inside: paperwork under load.

Hormuz is the modern Ugarit, and the load is showing in four channels at once.

The physical channel. Twenty million barrels a day became seven. The buffers that absorbed the first shock — strategic reserves, rerouted tonnage, the shadow fleet's elasticity — are thinning, and buffers are what you have before you have a crisis.

The narrowness is not only adversarial. On September third the Panama Canal Authority cut daily transit slots to nine Neopanamax and twenty-five Panamax, citing reduced precipitation in the watershed. No missiles, no proxies, no doctrine. Rainfall. Two of the world's critical maritime corridors constricted in the same week for entirely unrelated reasons, one hostile and one climatic. That coincidence may be the most useful fact in this dispatch, because it separates the variable from the actor. The adversary is not what makes a chokepoint dangerous. The chokepoint is what makes the adversary dangerous.

The financial channel. A hundred and two dollars is not the price of oil. It is the price of uncertainty about oil — compounded by war-risk premiums, by the supplemental funding the Pentagon will need to sustain the patrol posture, by jet fuel that has already pushed carriers to trim capacity and raise fares. Gasoline averaged four dollars and fifteen cents a gallon nationally on the ninth, after setting a Labor Day record at roughly the same level, about a dollar above last September. The household experiences the chokepoint as a price. The system experiences it as coupling.

The subsea channel. This one announced itself on Tuesday. The Revolutionary Guards said they had captured an American uncrewed submarine at the mouth of the strait, published video, and identified it as a Dive-LD — a nineteen-foot autonomous vehicle built by Anduril, ten-day endurance, six-thousand-meter depth rating, whose stated mission set includes mine countermeasures, seabed mapping, and the inspection of underwater cables and pipelines.

Set aside for a moment who is telling the truth about how it was acquired. The asset class is the news. A cable-and-pipeline inspection vehicle operating at Hormuz means the contested layer has moved below the waterline, into infrastructure that has no flag painted on it, no crew to rescue, and no established convention for what constitutes an attack. Three channels was the map this project has been using since Volume Two. It needs a fourth.

The narrative channel. And here the same object does a second job. Tehran called the vehicle a war trophy from a complex intelligence operation. A Pentagon spokesman said it had malfunctioned more than a day earlier, was an older model, and carried no sensitive data or classified sonar; a Navy captain at CENTCOM described it as dead in the water. Anduril confirmed the loss and noted that the Dive-LD is attritable — built from the outset for environments where losing one is an expected outcome. Reuters could not independently verify any of it. Outside analysts identified the hull from photographs by its flood holes, its fastenings, and a small transparent mast forward.

One object. Two complete and internally coherent framings. No neutral party able to adjudicate between them, and both attached — this is the part that matters — to a physical thing visible in a photograph rather than to an abstraction. When the narrative channel is under load, the contested material stops being interpretation and becomes evidence. Everyone can see the drone. Nobody can see what it means.

The adversary is not what makes a chokepoint dangerous. The chokepoint is what makes the adversary dangerous. — Two corridors, one week
Chapter III

The Proxy Surface and the Second-Order Map

The Houthis are the visible relay: Jazan, the Saudi facilities, the Red Sea transits. That much is legible and gets reported.

The second-order effect does not. Somali piracy has returned, and it is no longer a forecast. At least thirteen ships have been attacked since the start of the year. Two cargo vessels were seized in the Gulf of Aden within four days. The International Maritime Bureau logged twenty-four incidents in the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden between April and July, and three of the four vessels seized in that window were oil-product tankers. Up to ten million dollars was demanded for one of them. A Chinese vessel was released in March after a ransom reported at one and a half million.

The mechanism is a relay, not a coincidence. Matt Reisener of the Center for Maritime Strategy has described it plainly: Houthi missile and drone activity pushed commercial traffic closer to the Somali coastline, directly through waters where pirate groups operate; high energy prices simultaneously made those hulls and cargoes far more valuable; and naval assets that had spent a decade on counter-piracy were reassigned to counter-missile. Corey Ranslem of Dryad Global has raised the further possibility of technology transfer between the Houthis and al-Shabaab. Yemen's foreign-minister-designate, Afrah Al-Zouba, has said the Houthis are copying the Iranian model with the aim of closing two straits rather than one.

The Late Qing state offers the closest analogue. Its collapse was not primarily a matter of losing battles. It was the loss of the ability to police its own periphery, which created a standing market for private violence that then had to be bought off, subcontracted, or ignored. The periphery did not rebel. It monetized the gap. Every naval hour moved from piracy to missiles is a hull-hour of coverage sold to someone else, and the price of that coverage is now being set in the Gulf of Aden by people with no stake whatsoever in the war that opened the market.

Chapter IV

What This Is Not

The Collapse Signature framework tracks three variables in conjunction: chokepoint-throughput concentration, rising-narrative commitment, and institutional-adaptation velocity. One or two of these is ordinary. Modern states run on chokepoints and always have. The signature is the conjunction, and I have been careful in previous dispatches not to claim it where it isn't.

I would have written, a week ago, that the conjunction is present on the energy layer and that the institutional layer hasn't moved. That is no longer true, and the correction is the most important thing in this dispatch.

The Federal Reserve is about to raise rates into a supply shock. Policy has been held at three and a half to three and three-quarters percent for five consecutive meetings, and the July vote was nine to three, with Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan dissenting for a quarter-point increase. Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks moved market pricing from no-move-until-December to a live September hike. August CPI lands days before the decision. The S&P entered the month at 7,631; the ten-year is at 4.80.

The repricing has a proximate cause. On September fourth, nonfarm payrolls came in at a hundred sixty-two thousand against a Dow Jones consensus of fifty-three thousand. The unemployment rate held at four point one percent, and June and July were revised up by a combined fifty-five thousand — July flipping from a reported loss to a gain. Bars and restaurants led the hiring. Information-sector employment fell. Traders raised their bets on a September hike within hours of the release.

The same morning, the Bureau of Labor Statistics published its preliminary annual benchmark. Total nonfarm employment for the twelve months through March had been overcounted by seventy-nine thousand — the smallest such revision since 2021, and the seventh downward preliminary benchmark in eight years. The Bloomberg survey median had called for an upward revision of a hundred eighty-three thousand. The professional consensus missed the direction by a quarter of a million jobs.

Put those two releases side by side. What landed four days before a rate decision was a strong reading of the labor market, produced by an instrument with a documented and persistent one-way bias, at the precise moment the forecasting community had been shown to be off by a quarter-million in the wrong direction. Nobody involved is confused about this. The benchmark methodology is public and the revision history is a published table. The decision proceeds anyway, because a central bank that waited for clean measurement would never act at all.

And Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has objected on the record, on CNBC, on the grounds that this is a supply shock and you do not tighten into one absent evidence of second- and third-order effects. The President called the payroll figure a great jobs number and demanded cuts rather than hikes. That is three institutions, three readings of the same week's data, and one scheduled decision to settle it.

Which is the institutional-adaptation variable with a date attached. The question this dispatch would otherwise have to leave open — whether the paperwork is being written by people who believe the system absorbs this, or by people who suspect it can't — is currently being argued in public, on the record, with a nine-to-three vote already on the board behind it. That is a system in the middle of deciding, which is the more informative state and the rarer one to catch.

On the capital layer I will decline to assert anything. Oracle reports fiscal first-quarter results tomorrow after the close. Remaining performance obligations went from five hundred fifty-three billion to six hundred thirty-eight billion over the last fiscal year; the stock is down about twenty-one percent year to date; its credit default swap spread set a record in early August; the options market is pricing a double-digit move. Whatever the concentration risk is doing, it will say so tomorrow, and this dispatch is filed today. I would rather date-stamp the gap than fill it.

Chapter V

The Reading, Held Loosely

MARAD advisory 2026-004 — Persian Gulf, Strait of Hormuz, Gulf of Oman, Iranian attacks on commercial vessels — expires today. The Red Sea advisory runs through the twenty-second. The Black Sea advisory runs through the fifteenth.

Whether 2026-004 is renewed, quietly extended, or allowed to lapse will not be reported anywhere. It is a form with a date on it, produced by people under load, and it encodes what one part of the institution actually believes about its own exposure — as distinct from what any part of it says. It is the closest thing this month has to an Ugarit tablet, and it is publicly available.

The tankers are the story everyone can see. The price is the story everyone can price. The advisory is the paperwork, and the paperwork is where the collapse signature has always shown up first.
